# About Myself

I'm a father and a husband, an avid endurance sport enthusiast. This post is more about my professional journey, and how that experience relates to the other things I've written as blog posts.

### Origins

I did not graduate with a CS degree, however, even back in 2016, it was clear that every engineering profession are going to be coding if you want to be good at it. And I'm drawn the deepest, writing many simulation and web software for Duke Robotics. I then graduated into a Data Scientist/Engineer for AT&T, where I stayed one year.

### AWS

I joined AWS in 2019 and over the past 7 year has worked for 3 different teams - AWS RAM, AWS Service Quotas, and AWS Systems Manager. Each of them are platform services that interact with both customers and other services extensively inside Amazon. The first two are relatively regular AWS Services and control planes, the last is a rarity in AWS - a client side agent that powers the SSM management capability. I got to look at all different side of software engineering, from large scale services to client packaging and releases. And since 2025 I've been one of the highest consumer of tokens - but primarily not to work on the AI related things, but rather utilizing them to do other things - actual software.

I've seen the good, the bad and the ugly, and quite a number of them maybe different from general expectation or software engineering orthodoxy.

### Career Path

I'm lucky in meeting some of the best managers in AWS, and having been voluntold to move teams each time, i.e. "this team needs help, come", did not result in the normal team transition delay in terms of career. They eventually become plans, benefit multiple teams and even just tooling that are widely used without a formal resource allocation gets recognition. I've been to DevCons with my tooling, and my harness setup that I've developed for personal use, but in a customizable open ecosystem has been adopted by 100s.

### Culture

In AWS, culture is very much team specific, and governed by an overarching but sometimes conflicting leadership principles. Different teams may reflect different part of the leadership principle in addition to personalities of engineers and managers in those teams. This is also the hardest thing to get right or to change. It's easy to reduce the SEV2 count by investing in operations, unblock pipelines, but it's a lot harder to change the modus operandi of an existing team. On the very short term, this is not immediately visible upon deliveries, however, it manifest in decisions that compound over years, if left unchecked.

There are a lot of nuance here to expand into a post itself, but in gist, being twice the voluntold firefighter, I see cultural issues manifest the most when the teams become too scared to do things without bureaucracy. This bureaucracy can take shape of a person, a sacred ritual from someone who left, and being scared to take responsibility. It takes strong judgement and courage to take responsibility to fix things proactively and deploy it at the first moment, but if teams do not ever practice this, or have someone start doing it, these cultural elements are not trained, and they need to be just like other technical areas of software engineering.

### Product

Perhaps this is unique to cloud services, but from my observation, the best kind of product that succeed with customer is the one that allow the customer to do what they want. Not what you think they want. And having been through meetings with significant customer, their wish on the paper are likely in a similar situation. They think it is what they wanted, but upon implementation, will find that an alternative way works better. I'm not a product person, but my simple rule of product is if you build something that people can build upon, they would come. The delightful customer meeting usually ends with us telling them "you can already do this by step A,B,C" when they thought they had to ask for a new feature/product.

This will likely to be more prominent going forward, where wrappers for convenience can be replaced by agents. Customer have those too, we just need to ensure they know how to use us.

### Engineering Simplicity

Having seen many teams, both the ones I worked on and dozens of other AWS team that I personally interfaced with as a result of working on platform/base services. I can confidently say that one of the biggest resource cost of engineering is unnecessary complexity. Some of the systems with the biggest scale are surprisingly simple and brute-force-esque, which has its own beauty. However, others with much smaller scale sometimes go through the complexity gauntlet with industry "best practices" for scaling. Which led to higher cost in maintenance and infrastructure.

Just because it is on a book, does not mean it's the best idea. The best idea usually come from deep thinking of tradeoffs for that particular problem and not prescribed.
